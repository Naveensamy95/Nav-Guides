Pensions

Your pension explained

A pension is a long-term savings wrapper with powerful tax advantages. The trade-off: you can't touch it until you're nearly 60. There are two fundamentally different types — and it's crucial to know which one you have.

Defined Benefit (DB)

Employer guarantees income

You are promised a specific income in retirement regardless of how investments perform. The pension fund carries all the risk — not you. Common in the public sector, NHS, teachers, civil service.

DC / Workplace Pension

The modern default — most people have this
"A pot that grows based on what goes in and how investments perform. Your employer legally has to top it up — that's free money you shouldn't leave behind."

Auto-enrolled since 2012. Your contributions + employer contributions go into a fund. You typically choose a risk level (cautious/balanced/adventurous) and the provider invests for you. At retirement: 25% tax-free, rest as taxable income.

SIPP

Self-Invested Personal Pension
"The government pays 20–45p for every £1 you contribute — instantly. £800 in becomes £1,000 in your SIPP the same day. The catch: locked until age 57."

Unlike a workplace pension, you choose every investment yourself. Full control — shares, ETFs, bonds, funds. Basic rate taxpayers get 20% added automatically; higher rate taxpayers claim 40% back via self-assessment.

DB Pension

Defined Benefit — the gold standard
"You're promised £20,000/year in retirement for life. The pension fund invests, and if it falls short, your employer makes up the gap. You carry zero investment risk."

Formula: salary × years of service × accrual rate. Common in NHS, teachers, civil service. Large pensions companies administer these funds — investing contributions to meet all future payment promises. If the fund underperforms, that's a pension deficit.

What is a pension deficit?

If the fund's investments don't grow enough to pay all promised incomes, the gap is a deficit. The employer must top it up. In 2022, the gilt crisis created sudden liquidity crises in many UK DB funds — the Bank of England had to intervene.

VCT & EIS

Tax-incentivised startup investing — for higher earners
"The government gives you 30–50% of your investment back as a tax rebate — because you're taking on the risk of investing in small, early-stage UK businesses that most won't."

EIS (Enterprise Investment Scheme) gives 30% income tax relief on up to £1m/year. SEIS (Seed EIS) gives 50% on up to £200k/year in very early-stage companies. VCTs invest in a portfolio of small companies — 30% relief, tax-free dividends. All high-risk and illiquid.

State pension

The state pension

Completely separate from your workplace pension and your own investments. It's a government payment that starts at retirement age — but it's not a pot with your name on it.

"The state pension is not a savings account. There is no pot of money growing with your name on it. The government pays today's retirees using today's workers' National Insurance contributions. It's a promise from the state, not a personal investment."

You earn the right to it through National Insurance (NI) contributions — paid automatically from your salary, or credited if you're claiming child benefit, carer's allowance, or certain other benefits. Every qualifying year builds up your entitlement.

The full new state pension in 2025/26 is £221.20 per week — roughly £11,500 per year. It rises every year under the triple lock (see right). State pension age is currently 66 for men and women, rising to 67 between 2026 and 2028.

How National Insurance works Qualifying years

A qualifying year is any tax year in which you earned above the Lower Earnings Limit (~£6,396) and paid NI — or received NI credits. You don't need to work the full year.

NI credits are given automatically in some situations: receiving Child Benefit for a child under 12, being on certain disability or carer benefits, or being made redundant and claiming Jobseeker's Allowance.

You can check your NI record at gov.uk to see exactly how many qualifying years you have and whether you have any gaps worth filling.

GOV.UK: National Insurance explained

The triple lock guarantee

Inflation (CPI) — rises with the cost of living

Average earnings growth — rises with wage increases

2.5% — a guaranteed minimum increase each year

The state pension rises by whichever of these three is highest each April. This means it keeps pace with — and often exceeds — inflation over time.

Filling gaps in your NI record Worth checking

If you have gaps — years abroad, career breaks, self-employment where you didn't pay enough NI — you can often buy them back for a relatively small sum. The payback period is usually just a few years of extra state pension payments.

Voluntary NI contributions (Class 3) currently cost around £824 per missing year and add approximately £328/year to your state pension for life. That's a roughly 2.5-year payback — excellent value for most people.

GOV.UK: Voluntary NI contributions

⚠️ The state pension alone is not enough

£11,500/year is a meaningful base — but most people spend £25,000–£35,000/year for a moderate retirement lifestyle. The state pension covers roughly a third to a half of that. The rest must come from your workplace pension, SIPP, ISA, or other savings.

The state pension should be thought of as a foundation — not a retirement plan on its own.

Stocks & Shares ISA

The most important wrapper for self-directed investing
"A tax-free box. You put money in, buy investments inside it, and everything that grows — dividends, capital gains — the taxman can't touch. Same investments outside this box would cost you 10–20% CGT. Inside: zero."

Up to £20,000 per tax year. No tax on any growth, ever. Withdraw any time. The most important thing most people never fully use.

Key rule

You can split the £20,000 across multiple ISA types in the same tax year — e.g. £10k in a Stocks & Shares ISA and £10k in a Cash ISA. But the total cannot exceed £20,000.

What happens when you pay in monthly — step by step
1
Your £200/month lands in the ISA cash account

Sits as cash until the platform's next scheduled buy date — usually weekly or monthly depending on your settings.

2
Platform buys fund units at that day's price

If a global index fund costs £12.40 per unit, your £200 buys ~16.1 units. This price is the NAV — Net Asset Value.

3
Your unit count grows every month

Month 2: another ~16 units. Month 3: another ~16. Over years your unit count builds up. If the unit price also rises, both factors grow your pot.

4
Dividends buy more units automatically

Companies inside the fund pay profits. In an accumulation fund this immediately buys more units — you own more without paying in anything extra.

5
To get cash out, you sell (redeem) units

The platform sells your units at the current price. If the price has risen since you bought, you profit. Cash in your bank in 2–4 days — completely tax-free inside an ISA.

Where does the money actually go inside an index fund?
🌍
Your money buys a slice of thousands of companies
When you invest in a global index fund like Vanguard FTSE All-World, your £200 is spread across ~3,700 companies in 50 countries — Apple, Shell, Samsung, Nestlé, HSBC and thousands more. You own a tiny fraction of all of them.
£200 in FTSE All-World → ~£8 in Apple, ~£3 in Microsoft, ~£1.50 in Amazon, and so on across 3,700 companies
⚖️
The fund rebalances automatically
As companies grow or shrink, the index adjusts. Apple becomes worth more → it gets a bigger slice of your fund. A company collapses → it shrinks or disappears. You don't manage any of this — the fund does it automatically for a fee of around 0.2%/year.
A £10 annual fee on a £5,000 pot — far cheaper than any active fund manager
📬
Dividends flow back to you (or reinvest)
The companies inside the fund pay dividends (profit share). The fund collects these and either pays them to you as cash (income units) or uses them to buy more shares in the fund (accumulation units). Accumulation is better for long-term growth — it compounds.
Acc units: dividends silently buy more of the fund. Inc units: dividends paid to your cash account.
💰
Your unit price rises with the underlying companies
If the 3,700 companies collectively become 10% more valuable, your unit price rises 10%. Your units are worth more. You haven't received any cash — this is an unrealised gain. It only becomes real money when you sell.
Bought 100 units at £10 = £1,000. Fund rises 20%. Units now worth £12 each. Your 100 units = £1,200.

Funds inside wrappers — the same investment, three different tax outcomes

Here's the key insight most people miss: a pension fund and an ISA can hold exactly the same investments — the same global index fund, the same ETF, the same shares. What changes is the wrapper around them. The wrapper determines the tax treatment, the access rules, and who puts money in. The fund inside does not care which wrapper it sits in.

That same Vanguard FTSE All-World ETF bought inside a SIPP gets 20–45% tax relief on the money going in, grows tax-free, and then gets taxed as income on the way out at retirement. Inside an ISA: no relief in, but zero tax ever on the way out. In a GIA: no benefits at all, but no limits or lock-in either. Same 3,700 companies. What about DB pensions — what do they invest in?

In a DB pension, you don't choose the investments — the pension fund managers do. Your organisation (like the one you work for) pools all members' contributions and invests the whole pot across multiple asset types. The goal is to generate enough return to meet every member's promised income in retirement.

A typical large UK DB fund might allocate roughly like this:

Global equities
30–40%
UK / overseas bonds
25–35%
Gilts (LDI)
15–25%
Property / alternatives
10–15%
Hedge funds
5–10%
Cash
2–5%

You receive the promised income regardless of how this performs — but if the fund significantly underperforms, the employer faces a deficit and must top it up. This is why DB pension funds are some of the largest investors in global markets, and why the organisations managing them (like your employer) care deeply about investment strategy.

What this means if you're investing in an ISA or pension right now

The biggest risk for most people is paralysis — waiting for the "right time" to invest. Studies consistently show that time in the market beats timing the market. If you're investing monthly into a global index fund inside an ISA, short-term market noise matters very little over a 10–30 year horizon. For pension savers in DB schemes, higher gilt yields have generally improved funding positions — lower deficits, more stable promises.

Glossary

The words you need

Plain English. No jargon left unexplained.

State pension
A regular government payment starting at age 66, based on your NI record. Not a pot — a promise paid from current workers' NI contributions. Full amount ~£11,500/year (2025/26).
Triple lock
The guarantee that the state pension rises each April by the highest of: inflation, average earnings growth, or 2.5%. Protects pensioners' purchasing power over time.
NI qualifying year
A tax year in which you earned enough to pay National Insurance, or received NI credits. You need 35 qualifying years for the full state pension, 10 for any at all.
ETF
Exchange-Traded Fund. A basket of assets (all FTSE 100 shares) you buy and sell like a single share. Low cost, instant diversification.
Index fund
A fund that mirrors a market index automatically — no manager picking stocks. Very low fees. The evidence-based default for most investors.
NAV (unit price)
Net Asset Value. Total value of a fund's investments divided by number of units. The price you pay per unit when buying or selling.
Accumulation units
Fund units where dividends are automatically reinvested to buy more units. Better for long-term growth. Unit price rises faster than income units.
Income units
Fund units where dividends are paid to you as cash. Unit price stays lower. Preferred by retirees who need regular cash income.
Capital gain
Profit from selling an investment for more than you paid. Tax-free inside an ISA or pension. Taxed at 10–20% in a GIA above the £3,000 annual allowance.
Dividend
A cash payment companies make to shareholders from profits. Quarterly or annual. Tax-free inside an ISA.
Compound growth
Returns earning returns. £1,000 at 7% → £1,070. Next year 7% of £1,070, not £1,000. The snowball that makes early investing so powerful.
Redeem
Sell your fund units back to the fund at the current NAV price. The fund sells underlying investments to pay you cash. Takes 2–4 working days.
Annuity
A product bought at retirement that converts your DC pension pot into guaranteed income for life. Trades flexibility for certainty.
Drawdown
Keeping your pension pot invested and taking income gradually. Flexible but the pot can run out if markets fall or you live longer than expected.
Gilt
UK government bond. You lend money to the government at a fixed rate. Very safe. Returns lower than equities long-term but more stable.
LDI
Liability-Driven Investment. Used by DB pension funds to match assets to future liabilities. Central to the 2022 gilt crisis that froze several large UK funds.
Pension deficit
When a DB pension fund's investments aren't worth enough to cover all its promised future payments. The employer must make up the gap.
Accrual rate
In a DB pension, the fraction of salary earned as annual pension per year of service. 1/60th = 30 years gives half your salary as annual retirement income.
Leverage
Borrowing money to invest more than you own. Amplifies both gains and losses. Used by hedge funds — a 10x leveraged position loses 100% if trade moves 10% against it.
